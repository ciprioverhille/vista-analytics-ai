How to analyse a football match video without a video analyst

Most coaches film their matches. Very few have the time to watch them properly. Here is a method that fits into a normal week.

31 October 20266 min readCiprio Verhille

In professional football, a match is broken down by a team of analysts before the players are back in the dressing room. In amateur and semi-pro football, the footage usually ends up on a hard drive, watched once in fast-forward, if at all. Not because coaches don't care, but because watching 90 minutes properly takes three or four hours.

The good news: you don't need to watch everything. You need to watch the right things, with a clear question in mind. Here is how.

1. Start from your game plan, not from the video

The biggest mistake is to press play and "see what happens". You will end up commenting on the goals and the big chances, which you already remember.

Before opening the file, write down the three or four instructions you gave before the match. For example:

  • Press within five seconds after losing the ball.
  • Play out short from the goalkeeper.
  • Keep the distance between defence and midfield under 30 metres.

These become your viewing checklist. Every clip you look at should answer one of these questions.

2. Watch by phase, not in order

Instead of watching the match from start to finish, jump to the moments that matter for each instruction:

  • Turnovers for your pressing instruction: what happens in the five seconds after you lose the ball?
  • Goal kicks and restarts for your build-up instruction.
  • Defensive phases for your block: pause the video and look at the shape.

Write down the timestamp of every situation. Twenty well-chosen situations tell you more than ninety minutes of passive viewing.

3. Count, don't guess

"We pressed well in the first half" is an impression. "We pressed within five seconds on 12 of our 17 turnovers before half-time" is a fact you can show to your players.

A simple tally is enough: for each instruction, count how many times the situation happened, and how many times the team did what you asked. That ratio is the most useful number in your debrief.

A ratio turns an opinion into a conversation. Players argue with impressions; they rarely argue with clips.

4. Look at the timeline

Most plans don't fail for 90 minutes. They fade. Split your tally into periods of 15 minutes and you will often see exactly when the team stopped applying an instruction: after a goal conceded, after a substitution, or simply when legs got tired.

That moment is the most valuable information for your next training session.

5. Finish with individual feedback

Once the team picture is clear, go back to the players who had specific instructions. A winger asked to attack the box, a full-back asked to overlap: count their situations the same way, and pick two clips per player, one where it worked and one where it didn't.

6. Prepare a short debrief

Your players don't need your full notes. A good debrief fits on one page:

  1. What worked, with one clip.
  2. What didn't, with one clip.
  3. One priority for the week.
